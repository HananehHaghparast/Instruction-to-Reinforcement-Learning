# Instruction-to-Reinforcement-Learning
 Q-Learning, SARSA, and Policy Iteration

This repository contains two Jupyter notebooks I wrote for a reinforcement learning assignment. Both notebooks implement classical RL methods from scratch and analyze the results carefully. The first notebook trains Q-Learning and SARSA agents on the Taxi-v4 environment and compares them under two exploration schedules. The second notebook solves the Jack's Car Rental MDP problem using policy iteration, in a setting where both transitions and rewards are stochastic.

##  Q-Learning and SARSA on Taxi-v4

The Taxi-v4 environment puts an agent in a 5×5 grid where it must pick up a passenger at one location and drop them off at another. The state space has 500 discrete states and the action space has 6 actions (four movement directions, pickup, and drop-off). The reward is −1 per step, +20 for a successful drop-off, and −10 for an illegal pickup or drop-off, with a 200-step episode limit. Because the state space is small and discrete, tabular Q-learning is a natural fit.

The notebook implements two temporal-difference control algorithms from scratch. Q-Learning is off-policy: its update uses the maximum Q-value over the next state's actions, so it learns the optimal greedy policy regardless of the behavior policy actually followed. SARSA is on-policy: its update uses the Q-value of the action actually chosen at the next state, so it learns the value of the policy it is really executing, including its exploratory random actions. Both updates mask the bootstrapping term when an episode ends by termination rather than truncation, which matters for correctness but is easy to get wrong.

The two agents are each combined with two ε-greedy exploration schedules, giving four combinations. Fixed ε-greedy keeps ε = 0.1 throughout training. Decaying ε-greedy starts at ε = 1.0 (fully random) and decays exponentially at a rate of 0.995 down to a floor of 0.01. Each combination is trained four separate times with 500, 1000, 3000, and 5000 episodes, giving 16 trained agents in total. After training, each agent's greedy policy is evaluated on 20 fresh episodes to measure average return, average episode length, and success rate.

The results are quite clear. With only 500 episodes, all agents perform poorly — the decaying agents achieve just 5% success and the fixed-ε agents never finish an episode. At 1000 episodes, Q-Learning with decay already reaches 35% success while SARSA with decay reaches 20%, reflecting the advantage of off-policy learning. By 3000 episodes, both decaying agents hit 100% success with an optimal average episode length of about 14 steps, while the fixed-ε agents lag behind at 95%. Only at 5000 episodes do all four agents converge to the optimal solution. The notebook includes moving-average plots of return and episode length for each training budget, side-by-side bar charts of the evaluation metrics, and animated greedy rollouts so you can watch the trained agents navigate the grid.

The main conclusions are that decaying ε-greedy converges far faster than a fixed ε, because a permanent exploration rate keeps the Q-values from settling on the optimal policy; and that Q-Learning learns somewhat faster than SARSA at intermediate budgets, because it updates toward the greedy next action rather than the noisy action the behavior policy happened to choose. By 5000 episodes, both methods find the same optimum, so the difference is really about sample efficiency. The notebook answers three analysis questions at the end, asking for these comparisons and for a recommendation, which is Q-Learning with decaying ε-greedy.

## Policy Iteration for the Car Rental Problem

The second notebook solves a version of Jack's Car Rental, a classic stochastic MDP. Two garages each hold up to 20 cars. Each day, customers request rentals at a Poisson-distributed rate (λ = 3 for garage one, λ = 4 for garage two), earning 10 Rials per rented car, and cars are returned at Poisson rates of 3 and 2 respectively. Requests that cannot be served because no car is available are lost. Each night, up to 5 cars can be moved between garages at a cost of 2 Rials per car, and the discount factor is 0.9. The state is the pair of car counts at the two garages, giving a 21×21 state space. As suggested, Poisson probabilities are summed over n = 0 to 11.

The implementation uses policy iteration, which alternates between two steps. Policy evaluation repeatedly applies the Bellman expectation equation for the current policy, updating every state's value in place until the maximum change falls below a threshold. The expected return function is the careful part: it must marginalize over the joint distribution of rental requests and returns at both garages, clip available cars appropriately, cap capacities at 20, charge the move cost based only on the number of cars actually moved, and add the discounted expected value of the resulting next state. Policy improvement then sweeps every state, tests every valid action (bounded by both the number of cars available and the maximum of five), and replaces the policy with the greedy choice. A small tolerance is used when comparing action values so floating-point noise does not cause the policy to thrash between equivalent actions.

The algorithm converges in five iterations. The optimal policy follows the intuitive structure: when one garage has many more cars than the other, it pays to move up to five cars from the fuller garage toward the one with higher demand, and when the two garages are reasonably balanced, it is optimal to do nothing and avoid paying the move cost. The notebook plots the policy as a heatmap over the state space, and plots the converged value function, which is highest around the region where both garages hold roughly eight to ten cars — the balance where expected demand and supply meet most efficiently.

## Structure of the Repository

- `qlearning_sarsa.ipynb`: environment inspection, ε-greedy action selection, ε schedules, training functions for Q-Learning and SARSA, policy evaluation, experiment runner over four episode counts, summary tables, training curves, bar charts, animated demonstrations, and written answers to three analysis questions.
- `MDP.ipynb`: environment constants, cached Poisson probabilities, expected return computation, policy evaluation, policy improvement, policy iteration loop, and plots of the optimal policy and value function.


## What I Took Away From This

The first notebook shows that the choice of exploration schedule matters as much as the choice of algorithm: a decaying ε made a far bigger difference than Q-Learning versus SARSA did. It also shows the theoretical off-policy advantage of Q-Learning in practice, though the difference shrinks as training lengthens. The second notebook shows how exact dynamic programming works when the transition model is fully known: with careful expectation computation, policy iteration finds a clean, interpretable optimal policy for a genuinely stochastic problem in just a handful of iterations. Together the two notebooks cover both model-free and model-based approaches to the same underlying goal — finding good behavior in a Markov decision process.
